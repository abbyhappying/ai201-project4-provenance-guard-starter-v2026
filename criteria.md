# Acceptance criteria — Provenance Guard

Five criteria that say what "working" means for this service, written in unit 7
**before** it was built. All five are mine. Each names a number or something
plainly observable, and each has a sentence on why that number.

Unit 8 runs **three trials**. Criteria 1, 2, 3 and 5 are required to hold in
**each of the three trials**. Criterion 4 is a route-level check run **once by
hand with `curl`**, because `run_eval.py` only calls `/submit`.

**Minimum text length (used by the contract and criterion 5):** `/submit`
accepts text of **at least 20 words and at most 20,000 characters**; anything
shorter or longer is `400 invalid_input`.

---

## 1. False positives — a real writer is rarely called AI

**Criterion:** In each of the 3 trials, **at most 1 of the 10 pieces I wrote
myself** comes back with `guess == "ai"`. At least 3 of those 10 are written in
a deliberately plain, second-language style (short sentences, common words, no
semicolons or dashes) — the writer the README's false-positive trace is about.
`"unsure"` does **not** count as a false positive.

**These 10 pieces are separate from the 5 human texts in criterion 2**, so no
piece is judged against two criteria at once.

**Why this target:** on a writing site a wrong accusation costs the writer a
lot; a missed AI text costs the site a little. I cap false positives at 1 in 10
per trial and accept that more AI text gets through. I include plain L2-style
pieces on purpose, because plain writing is exactly the blind spot of the model
signal, and testing only ordinary native writing would dodge the case the README
is built around.

*Implementation:* the 10 pieces live in `scenarios.py`; `run_eval.py` reports
`guess`.

---

## 2. Score spread — clearly-AI and clearly-human text separate

**Criterion:** The clearly-AI group is **5 texts generated by ChatGPT (GPT-4o),
October 2026**, from the prompts *"Write a 150-word product description for a
reusable water bottle"*, *"Write a 150-word explanation of how photosynthesis
works"*, *"Write a 150-word review of a local coffee shop"*, *"Write a 150-word
email asking a landlord to fix a leaking tap"*, and *"Write a 150-word summary
of the plot of a film you have seen"*. The clearly-human group is **5 texts I
write myself** on the same five topics, kept to a similar length (120–180 words)
and the same register.

In each of the 3 trials, the **lowest `combined_score` in the AI group is higher
than the highest `combined_score` in the human group** — zero overlap — and the
**mean `combined_score` of the AI group is at least 0.25 higher** than the mean
of the human group.

**Why this target:** if the scores cannot be separated, the thresholds mean
nothing. 0.25 on a 0–1 scale is the smallest gap I'd call "clearly apart", and
using a current chat model rather than GPT-2 keeps the test honest: GPT-2 scoring
its own output would push signal 1 near 1.0 automatically and show only that the
detector recognises GPT-2, not that it separates AI from human writing. Lengths
are held similar because type-token ratio falls as text gets longer, so a length
difference alone could fake a signal. Zero overlap rather than one allowed pair
is a deliberate choice: GPT-2 scoring *should* be deterministic (eval() mode, no
sampling; I will confirm this in the first trial), so the only real source of an
off-score is a single unusual text — and I would rather that text be visible as
a failure than be absorbed by an allowance.

*Implementation:* `run_eval.py` does not compute means; I read the results table
and compute the averages by hand (or with a short script).

---

## 3. Label coverage — all three labels are reachable, from the right inputs

**Criterion:** In each of the 3 trials:

- at least 1 input from the **clearly-human group** gets `guess == "human"`;
- at least 1 input from the **clearly-AI group** gets `guess == "ai"`;
- at least 2 of the **borderline inputs** get `guess == "unsure"`.

The clearly-human and clearly-AI groups are **the same 5 + 5 texts used in
criterion 2**. There are **5 borderline inputs**, each a **lightly edited copy of
one of the five AI texts from criterion 2** (a few words changed, one sentence
split), so this shows how little editing moves the score.

**Why this target:** this criterion is about whether each label can be reached,
not about accuracy — accuracy is covered by criteria 1 and 2. So "at least 1"
is a deliberate floor, not a low accuracy bar. The labels must come from the
group they describe: a human piece mislabelled `"ai"` must not count toward
showing `"ai"` is reachable. This criterion depends on the style signal actually
working: while it is the 0.5 stub, `"human"` is almost unreachable, which is the
point.

*Implementation:* nothing extra; `scenarios.py` holds the three groups.

---

## 4. The appeal path — an appeal always changes status and is recorded

**Criterion:** For **3 real** `content_id`s returned by `/submit`:
`POST /appeal` returns `200` with `status == "under_review"`; the audit log
gains an `appeal` entry for that `content_id`; and the original entry for that
`content_id` with `status == "decided"` is **still present and unchanged** —
same `guess`, same `combined_score`, same `timestamp`. For **3 made-up**
`content_id`s, `/appeal` returns `404 not_found`. For a request with missing
`reasoning`, `/appeal` returns `400 invalid_input`.

This is checked **once, by hand with `curl`** — it is not part of the three-trial
rule at the top, because `run_eval.py` only calls `/submit`.

**Why this target:** the appeal is the only exit from a false positive, so it
must always change status, always be recorded, and never overwrite the original
decision. The 404 is so a mistyped id gets a clear answer instead of a silent
"success". Three real and three fake ids is enough to test the rule rather than
one lucky case.

*Implementation:* `/appeal` is ~15–20 lines using `audit.entries_for` and
`audit.log_appeal`.

---

## 5. Bad input — bad requests get 400, never 500

**Criterion:** In each of the 3 trials, every request below returns
`400 invalid_input` with a body of the shape `{"error", "message"}`, and **no
response is a 500**. **In every case, all fields other than the one named are
valid**, so each 400 has exactly one cause:

- (a) `text` missing;
- (b) `text` is `""`;
- (c) `text` is 10 words (`"one two three four five six seven eight nine ten"`), i.e. under the 20-word minimum;
- (d) `text` is `"word " * 5000` (25,000 characters), over the 20,000 limit;
- (e) valid text but `creator_id` missing;
- (f) the request body is a JSON list, `[1, 2]`, not an object;
- (g) `text` is `null`;
- (h) `text` is `42`.

**Why this target:** a 500 tells the user "the site is broken"; a typed 400 with
an actionable sentence is the service doing its job. Cases (c) and (d) come
straight from the contract's 20-word minimum and 20,000-character limit. Cases
(f), (g) and (h) are real bugs in the starter, but they fail at different points:
a list body crashes at `payload.get(...)` because a list has no `.get`, while a
`null` or number `text` crashes later when the code calls `len()` or `.strip()`
on it. Malformed bodies like these are a common real failure, and `app.py`
already warns about list bodies; about 20 lines of checks at the top of
`/submit` keep them from producing 500s.

*Implementation:* input checks at the top of `/submit`; tested by `run_eval.py`
with a "bodies" scenario, or by `curl`.
